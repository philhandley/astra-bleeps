# Astra Bleeps: chiptune fact brief

Checked on 11 October 2026. Every fact below has a source page that was opened and read during this check. Numbers are copied from that page. Where sources disagree, both versions are given. Facts that could not be confirmed are listed at the end and should not be used in the app.

"Kid comparison" lines are suggestions for the app, not checked facts.

## 1. What "chiptune" means

- **Fact:** A chiptune is electronic music made with the sound chip of an old console, arcade machine or home computer, or music made to sound like one. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiptune **Kid comparison:** A song played using only the built-in beeps and buzzes of an old game machine.
- **Fact:** The name is said to come from the Amiga computer's "chip memory". Early tracker music with small samples came to be called "chiptunes" for that reason. **Number:** None. Wikipedia gives no date and does not say who first used the word. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiptune **Kid comparison:** Named after a little memory chip inside an Amiga computer.
- **Fact:** Wikipedia gives the cultural origin of chiptune as Japan in the late 1970s to early 1980s. The earliest tracker chiptunes are dated to 1989. **Number:** Late 1970s to early 1980s; 1989. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiptune **Kid comparison:** It started around the time of the arcade game Space Invaders (1978).

## 2. Nintendo Game Boy (DMG-01)

- **Fact:** The Game Boy launched in Japan on 21 April 1989. **Number:** 21 April 1989. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy **Kid comparison:** Its launch was about 37 years ago, roughly six times as long as a six-year-old has been alive.
- **Fact:** The Game Boy launched in North America on 31 July 1989 and in Europe on 28 September 1990. **Number:** 31 July 1989 (North America); 28 September 1990 (Europe). **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy **Kid comparison:** Europe got it about a year and a half after Japan.
- **Fact:** Nintendo's own sales table lists 118.69 million Game Boy hardware units sold worldwide, life to date, as of 30 June 2026. The table has one "Game Boy" line and no separate Game Boy Color line, so the figure covers the Game Boy family including Game Boy Color. **Number:** 118.69 million. **Source:** https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ir/en/finance/hard_soft/index.html **Kid comparison:** Counting one unit every second without stopping would take nearly four years. **Note:** The table numbers were read from the page's image text, so check the figure on the page by eye before it goes in the app. Wikipedia's infobox gives the same 118.69 million, citing Nintendo's 2016 figures. Its body text ties the same figure to 2003 (citing BusinessWeek). We have not resolved that conflict, so use Nintendo's table.
- **Fact:** The Game Boy has four sound channels: two pulse (square-wave) channels, one wave channel and one noise channel. **Number:** 4 channels (2 pulse, 1 wave, 1 noise). **Source:** https://gbdev.io/pandocs/Audio.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy **Kid comparison:** A four-piece band: two beepers, one custom-wave player and one drummer who makes hiss and crash sounds.
- **Fact:** The pulse channels have four fixed duty cycles, which set how much of each wave is "on": 12.5%, 25%, 50% and 75%. Pan Docs notes there is no audible difference between 25% and 75%. **Number:** 12.5%, 25%, 50%, 75%. **Source:** https://gbdev.io/pandocs/Audio_Registers.html **Kid comparison:** At 12.5% the sound is on for one eighth of each wave, so it is a thin, tiny beep. 50% is the fair half-and-half setting.
- **Fact:** The wave channel plays a 32-sample wave. Each sample is 4 bits, and the samples are stored in 16 bytes of wave RAM. Its volume has four settings: mute, 100%, 50% and 25%. **Number:** 16 bytes; 32 samples; 4 bits per sample; volumes 100%, 50%, 25%, mute. **Source:** https://gbdev.io/pandocs/Audio_Registers.html **Kid comparison:** A drawing of a wave made of 32 dots, each dot one of 16 heights.
- **Fact:** The noise channel makes random hiss-like sounds from a shift register that is either 15 bits or 7 bits wide, chosen by the player. The 7-bit setting sounds more regular, so some pitches sound more like a pulse than noise. **Number:** 15-bit or 7-bit. **Source:** https://gbdev.io/pandocs/Audio_Registers.html **Kid comparison:** Like shaking a box of dried peas with two different shaking patterns.

## 3. NES / Famicom

- **Fact:** The Famicom was released in Japan on 15 July 1983. **Number:** 15 July 1983. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Entertainment_System **Kid comparison:** It came out six years before the Game Boy.
- **Fact:** The NES had a test launch in New York on 18 October 1985, then a full North American release on 27 September 1986. Wikipedia also mentions a Los Angeles launch in February 1986. **Number:** 18 October 1985 (test launch); 27 September 1986 (full release). **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Entertainment_System **Kid comparison:** The first shops in New York got it in October 1985. **Note:** A search summary gave February 1986 as the full release. The article gives 27 September 1986, so use that.
- **Fact:** Europe got the NES first in Scandinavia on 1 September 1986 (the infobox date). The UK, Ireland, Italy and Australia got it in 1987. The article's body text says Europe got it "in early 1986" and that most European countries released it in 1987. **Number:** 1 September 1986 (Scandinavia); 1987 (UK). **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Entertainment_System **Kid comparison:** The UK got it about a year after Scandinavia.
- **Fact:** Nintendo's sales table lists 61.91 million Family Computer/NES units sold worldwide, life to date, as of 30 June 2026. Wikipedia's infobox cites Nintendo's consolidated sales for the same figure. **Number:** 61.91 million. **Source:** https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ir/en/finance/hard_soft/index.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Entertainment_System **Kid comparison:** About half as many consoles as the Game Boy family. **Note:** The same table caveat applies: the figure was read from the image text, so check it by eye.
- **Fact:** The NES sound unit (APU) is built into the console's processor and has five channels: two pulse, one triangle, one noise and one DPCM channel for playing back samples. **Number:** 5 channels. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Entertainment_System ; https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU **Kid comparison:** A five-piece band, where the fifth player can play recorded clips.
- **Fact:** Each pulse channel has four duty cycles (12.5%, 25%, 50%, 75%), a 16-level volume (4 bits, levels 0 to 15), and a sweep unit that can move the pitch up or down. **Number:** 4 duty cycles; 16 volume levels. **Source:** https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU_Pulse **Kid comparison:** A volume dial with 16 clicks.
- **Fact:** The triangle channel has no volume control. It steps through 32 fixed levels (each a 4-bit value), so it sounds stepped rather than smooth. **Number:** 32 steps; 4-bit values. **Source:** https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU **Kid comparison:** A staircase of 32 steps with no dimmer switch.
- **Fact:** The noise channel uses a 15-bit random-bit generator, called an LFSR (linear feedback shift register). In normal mode it repeats after 32,767 steps. In short mode, feedback comes from bit 6 instead of bit 1, which gives a short cycle of 93 or 31 steps and a buzzing tone. **Number:** 15-bit; 32,767 steps (normal); 93 or 31 steps (short). **Source:** https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU_Noise ; https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU **Kid comparison:** Normal mode is a long, random hiss. Short mode is a shorter, buzzier hiss.
- **Fact:** The DPCM channel plays back 1-bit delta-encoded samples through a 7-bit output counter. It is the only NES channel that plays back sampled sound. **Number:** 1-bit deltas; 7-bit counter. **Source:** https://www.nesdev.org/wiki/APU **Kid comparison:** A rough, grainy recording played through one of the instruments.

## 4. Commodore 64 and its SID chip

- **Fact:** The Commodore 64 was introduced in January 1982, first shown at CES, and went on sale in August 1982 at US$595. **Number:** January 1982 (introduced); August 1982 (sale); US$595. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64 **Kid comparison:** It arrived about a year and a half before the Famicom.
- **Fact:** The C64's sound chip is the MOS Technology 6581 SID (Sound Interface Device). A revised chip, the 8580 (sometimes labelled 6582), took over from the 6581 around 1986. **Number:** 6581 (1982); 8580 (around 1986). **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology_6581 **Kid comparison:** SID is short for "Sound Interface Device".
- **Fact:** Bob Yannes designed the SID. His small team (himself, two technicians and a CAD operator) designed and finished the chip in five months, in the second half of 1981. **Number:** Five months; second half of 1981. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology_6581 **Kid comparison:** About a school term and a half.
- **Fact:** The SID has three voices, and each voice has its own ADSR envelope (attack, decay, sustain, release) that shapes how a note fades in and out. **Number:** 3 voices. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology_6581 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64 **Kid comparison:** Three singers, each able to fade in and out on their own.
- **Fact:** Each voice can produce four waveforms: triangle, sawtooth, pulse (with adjustable width) and pseudorandom noise. **Number:** 4 waveforms. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology_6581 **Kid comparison:** Four sounds to choose from for each singer.
- **Fact:** The SID has a multimode resonant filter with low-pass, high-pass and band-pass outputs, and digitally controlled cutoff and resonance. **Number:** Three filter outputs. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology_6581 **Kid comparison:** A tone knob that can cut the bass or the treble.
- **Fact:** The SID was first designed to have 32 voices, but that could not be finished in time, so the design of one oscillator was copied three times. **Number:** 32 planned; 3 built. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOS_Technology_6581 **Kid comparison:** The designers planned a choir of 32 singers but ended up with three copies of one singer. **Note:** The Wikipedia article has maintenance tags, so treat this as a secondary account.
- **Fact:** Sales estimates for the C64 range from 12.5 million to 17 million units. Wikipedia says company sales records indicate about 12.5 million, and that claims of 17, 22 and 30 million have also been made. **Number:** 12.5 to 17 million (estimate range); about 12.5 million (company records). **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64 **Kid comparison:** Counting to 12.5 million, one number per second, would take about 145 days without a break.
- **Fact:** Guinness World Records lists the C64 as the best-selling desktop computer model of all time. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64 (citing Guinness World Records) **Kid comparison:** The best-selling desktop computer ever, according to Guinness.

## 5. Space Invaders (Taito, 1978)

- **Fact:** Space Invaders was developed by Taito and designed by Tomohiro Nishikado. It was released in Japan in July 1978 and in North America in November 1978. **Number:** July 1978; November 1978. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders **Kid comparison:** A game from 1978, about three years before the C64's SID chip was designed.
- **Fact:** The game's signature background music is a looping bassline of four notes. **Number:** 4 notes. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders **Kid comparison:** Four notes on repeat, like a tune you can hum over and over.
- **Fact:** As the invaders are defeated, their movement and the music speed up. The article says the hardware drew each frame faster when fewer aliens were on screen, and the designer kept this effect rather than correcting it. **Number:** Not a number. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders **Kid comparison:** The fewer aliens left, the faster the music, like a countdown getting quicker.
- **Fact:** The game historian Bill Loguidice compared the speeding-up background music to a heartbeat. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders **Kid comparison:** A heartbeat that goes faster when you are running or scared.
- **Fact:** The infobox credits Michiyuki Kamei with the audio. The article body says Kamei built the game's audio circuitry, but it does not say who wrote the bassline. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders **Kid comparison:** None needed.

## 6. Super Mario Bros. (1985) music, by Koji Kondo

- **Fact:** Koji Kondo, a Nintendo sound designer, wrote the Super Mario Bros. theme, officially called the "Ground Theme". **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme **Kid comparison:** The same person wrote one of the most famous game tunes ever.
- **Fact:** Kondo had four channels to work with: two pulse-wave channels, one triangle-wave channel and one noise channel. He worked out the melody in advance, moved it between the three melodic channels, and used the noise channel for the rhythm. **Number:** 4 channels. **Source:** https://blogs.loc.gov/now-see-hear/2023/08/from-the-national-recording-registry-super-mario-1985/ **Kid comparison:** A band of four players: two beepers, one softer round sound and one drummer. **Note:** The NES has five channels in all. The theme does not use the fifth (the DMC sample channel).
- **Fact:** The theme is in the key of C major and runs at 100 beats per minute. Kondo composed it using only a small keyboard. **Number:** 100 beats per minute. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme **Kid comparison:** At 100 beats per minute the beat comes about every 0.6 seconds, like a steady tap of your foot.
- **Fact:** The theme was included in the American National Recording Registry in 2023. **Number:** 2023. **Source:** https://blogs.loc.gov/now-see-hear/2023/08/from-the-national-recording-registry-super-mario-1985/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_Bros._theme **Kid comparison:** A library's list of important recordings.

## 7. Trackers, Ultimate Soundtracker, Little Sound DJ

- **Fact:** A music tracker is a sequencer in which notes sit in channels along a vertical timeline, in a grid of fixed time slots, and are typed on the keyboard. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_tracker **Kid comparison:** Like squared paper where you fill in one box at a time.
- **Fact:** In Furnace (a tracker), pressing a note key places the note at the cursor and moves the cursor to the next row. Rows are read in order down the screen. The Furnace page does not say "top to bottom" in so many words, so the direction is implied. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://mintlify.wiki/tildearrow/furnace/patterns/editing **Kid comparison:** Like reading a comic strip from top to bottom, one panel at a time.
- **Fact:** Ultimate Soundtracker was written by Karsten Obarski, a German software developer and composer, and released as a commercial product for the Amiga in December 1987. It mixed four channels and limited a song to 15 samples. **Number:** 1987; 4 channels; 15 samples. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultimate_Soundtracker **Kid comparison:** Four instruments playing at once, like four friends on one team. **Note:** The Music tracker article credits the release to EAS Computer Technik, while the Ultimate Soundtracker article names no publisher. Wikipedia also calls it the first music tracker, but we did not confirm that beyond Wikipedia.
- **Fact:** Little Sound DJ (LSDj) was created by Johan Kotlinski, who was first released in the year 2000 according to a May 2016 article on Synthtopia. **Number:** 2000. **Source:** https://www.synthtopia.com/?p=70755 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LSDJ (Wikipedia names Kotlinski as creator but gives no year). **Kid comparison:** A music program built into an old handheld that is about 26 years old.
- **Fact:** LSDj is a tracker for the Game Boy's four sound channels, making four-track music with the Game Boy's 4-bit sound. **Number:** 4 channels; 4 tracks. **Source:** https://www.synthtopia.com/?p=70755 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LSDJ **Kid comparison:** Four layers of music, like four layers of a sandwich.
- **Fact:** Musicians still make music with LSDj. The official Little Sound DJ site says it is "used by video game musicians, Grammy winners, and chiptune virtuosos". A 2016 Synthtopia article says it had "almost become the default software for making chiptune". **Number:** None. **Source:** https://www.littlesounddj.com/ ; https://www.synthtopia.com/?p=70755 **Kid comparison:** Lots of people still make songs on a Game Boy. **Note:** The first source is the developer's own site, and the second is from 2016. No independent 2025 or 2026 source was found.

## 8. Arpeggios

- **Fact:** On chips that could only play a few notes at once, composers played the notes of a chord one after another very fast on one channel. This is called an arpeggio, and the ear hears it as a chord. The paper calls the super-fast arpeggio the most recognised feature of this style of writing. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://www.ludomusicology.org/2015/07/16/compositional-strategies-for-programmable-sound-generators-with-limited-polyphony/ (Blake Troise, 16 July 2015) **Kid comparison:** Flicking through the notes of a chord so fast your ears think they are all playing together.
- **Fact:** The limit is on simultaneous voices (channels), not on how many notes can be played in sequence. The paper calls the sharing of channels to suggest more voices "false polyphony". **Number:** None. **Source:** https://www.ludomusicology.org/2015/07/16/compositional-strategies-for-programmable-sound-generators-with-limited-polyphony/ **Kid comparison:** The chip can only sing a few notes at the same moment, but it can sing lots of notes one after another.
- **Note:** The paper says the C64 had "four channels". Wikipedia says the SID has three voices. This brief uses three, from Wikipedia.

## 9. Sound science basics

- **Fact:** Sound is vibration that travels through the air. In air, sound is a compression wave. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound **Kid comparison:** Like squeezing a Slinky at one end and watching the squeeze travel along it.
- **Fact:** Humans hear frequency as pitch. Frequency is measured in hertz (Hz). The article defines one hertz as one event (or cycle) per second for periodic events. **Number:** 1 hertz = one cycle per second. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertz **Kid comparison:** One hertz is one wiggle per second, like one tick of a clock.
- **Fact:** Concert A (A4, the A above middle C) is 440 Hz. It is widely used as concert pitch in the UK and US. In continental Europe, A4 commonly ranges from 440 to 444 Hz. **Number:** 440 Hz; 440 to 444 Hz in continental Europe. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A440_(pitch_standard) **Kid comparison:** 440 wiggles every second, far too fast to count out loud.
- **Fact:** The human hearing range is commonly given as 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. Hearing varies between people, and the top end shrinks with age, usually starting around age eight. **Number:** 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_range **Kid comparison:** A 20,000-wiggle-per-second sound is a very high squeak that many grown-ups can no longer hear. **Note:** The Hertz article gives 20 Hz to 16,000 Hz for the average adult, which conflicts with the 20,000 figure. Use "20 Hz to 20,000 Hz" with the caveat that it varies.
- **Fact:** Sound travels at about 343 metres per second in air at 20 degrees Celsius, which is about 1,235 km/h. **Number:** 343 m/s; 1,235 km/h. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_sound **Kid comparison:** A thunderclap 3.4 km away takes about 10 seconds to reach you.

## 10. Waveform words

- **Fact (square / pulse):** A square wave is often described as sounding hollow, and it contains only odd harmonics. Apple's Logic Pro guide describes the square as "hollow and woody sounding", useful for basses, clarinets and oboes. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_wave_(waveform) ; https://help.apple.com/logicpro-instruments/mac/10.1/en.lproj/lgsi7581b8df.html **Kid comparison:** Hollow, like a clarinet.
- **Fact (triangle):** Apple describes the triangle as "sweet sounding, softer than sawtooth", useful for flutes and pads. Wikipedia says a triangle wave has only odd harmonics, and that they fall away much faster than a square wave's. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://help.apple.com/logicpro-instruments/mac/10.1/en.lproj/lgsi7581b8df.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_wave **Kid comparison:** Soft, like a flute. **Note:** The flute comparison comes only from Apple. Wikipedia does not describe the triangle's sound at all.
- **Fact (sawtooth):** A sawtooth wave contains all whole-number harmonics. Wikipedia describes its sound as "harsh and clear" and says it suits bowed string instruments. Apple describes it as "warm and even" and suggests it for strings, pads, bass and brass. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sawtooth_wave ; https://help.apple.com/logicpro-instruments/mac/10.1/en.lproj/lgsi7581b8df.html **Kid comparison:** Full and rich, like a string instrument being bowed. **Note:** The sources disagree on whether it sounds bright or warm. "Bright and buzzy like brass" is not confirmed.
- **Fact (noise):** White noise is heard as a hissing sound, and in music it is used to recreate percussion such as cymbals and snare drums. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_noise **Kid comparison:** A shh sound, like the rattle of a snare drum. **Note:** No opened source mentions explosions.
- **Fact (sine, for contrast):** Apple describes the sine as "a pure tone". **Number:** None. **Source:** https://help.apple.com/logicpro-instruments/mac/10.1/en.lproj/lgsi7581b8df.html **Kid comparison:** A smooth whistle.

## 11. Sound effects that borrow a music channel

- **Fact:** On the NES, sound effects take priority over music. In NES Elite, effects can use the two pulse channels and the noise channel (not the triangle), and the music routines let the effects take priority. The music on that channel drops out while the effect plays. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://elite.bbcelite.com/deep_dives/sound_effects_in_nes_elite.html **Kid comparison:** Like a fire alarm that makes the radio go quiet until the alarm stops. **Note:** This is a deep-dive into NES Elite, not Super Mario Bros.
- **Fact (Super Mario Bros.):** The jump sound is sent to the "square 1 sound-effect queue", so the jump takes the first pulse channel for a moment. The stomp sound uses the same queue. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://6502disassembly.com/nes-smb/SuperMarioBros.html (annotated disassembly, around addresses b500 to b51a) **Kid comparison:** The jump borrows the first beeping instrument for a moment. **Note:** The code that silences the music on the channel during the effect was not found in the parts of the disassembly that were read.
- **Fact (Super Mario Bros.):** The coin "grab" sound is sent to the Square 2 queue (around address bb79). The 1-up sound also goes to Square 2 (around bc20) and is played when the coin count reaches 100. **Number:** 100 coins for a 1-up. **Source:** https://6502disassembly.com/nes-smb/SuperMarioBros.html **Kid comparison:** Collect 100 coins and you get an extra life.
- **Game Boy:** No opened source confirms that Game Boy sound effects take over music channels. See the list of unconfirmed facts below.

## 12. Music that speeds up when time is running out

- **Fact (Super Mario Bros. timer):** In Super Mario Bros.'s code, the timer check queues a "time running out" music event when the timer reads 100. The check is "if timer not at 100, branch" and "otherwise load time running out music". The trigger is at 100, not below 100. **Number:** 100. **Source:** https://6502disassembly.com/nes-smb/SuperMarioBros.html (around addresses b769 to b784) **Kid comparison:** The hurry-up tune starts when the clock reaches 100 on its way down.
- **Fact (Super Mario Bros. timer length):** Each in-game timer count lasts 0.4 seconds, so the 100 mark comes with about 40 seconds left (100 × 0.4 s). **Number:** 0.4 seconds per count; about 40 seconds left. **Source:** https://www.mariowiki.com/Time_Limit (a fan wiki; the 0.4 s figure comes from that page). **Kid comparison:** About as long as it takes to count slowly to 40.
- **Fact (Super Mario Bros. tempo):** The Mario Wiki says the level music speeds up when the warning sounds. The disassembly sections that were read show a separate time-running-out tune, but no tempo change. The speed-up is therefore not confirmed in the code. **Source:** https://www.mariowiki.com/Time_Limit ; https://6502disassembly.com/nes-smb/SuperMarioBros.html
- **Fact (other games):** Fail Factory (Armature Studios) has three versions of each track, moderate, brisk and frantic, which play as the timer shortens. In Spyder (Sumo Digital), beeps and alarms go off rapidly in sync with the tempo in the final ten seconds. Both examples are from composer Winifred Phillips, in an article dated 15 December 2020. **Number:** Three versions; final ten seconds. **Source:** https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/video-game-music-composers-the-timed-challenge-pt-2- **Kid comparison:** Three versions of one song, from a walk to a run.
- **Fact (Space Invaders):** The music speeds up as the invaders are defeated, so it gets faster as fewer aliens remain. **Number:** None. **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Invaders **Kid comparison:** The fewer aliens left, the faster the music. Note: the "as they get closer" wording is not confirmed (see below).

## Facts we checked and could NOT confirm

Do not use these in the app until they are confirmed.

- **Space Invaders bassline goes down (descending):** The Arcade History page returned 403, and the Bradford College PDF could not be read. Wikipedia does not say whether the notes ascend or descend.
- **Space Invaders bassline is chromatic or diatonic:** No source with a note-by-note analysis was found.
- **Space Invaders music speeds up "as the aliens get closer":** Wikipedia says the speed-up is linked to aliens being defeated. The "closer" wording comes only from secondary sites that were not opened.
- **Sawtooth is "bright and buzzy like brass":** Apple says "warm and even" and suggests brass. Wikipedia says "harsh and clear". Only Apple mentions brass.
- **Triangle is "flute-like":** Only Apple says this, and Wikipedia does not describe the triangle's sound.
- **Square is "buzzy":** No source says this. Wikipedia and Apple both say hollow or woody.
- **Noise is used for explosions:** No opened source mentions explosions.
- **Game Boy sound effects take over music channels:** No opened source confirms this.
- **Super Mario Bros. silences the music on a channel during a sound effect:** The disassembly shows which queue each effect uses, but the code that silences the music was not found in the parts read.
- **Super Mario Bros. music speeds up when the timer warning plays:** Only the Mario Wiki says this. The disassembly showed a separate tune with no tempo change.
- **Super Mario Bros. 3 and Super Mario World hurry-up behaviour:** Only search summaries of Mario Wiki pages, which were not opened.
- **Little Sound DJ first released in 2000:** Only Synthtopia (2016) gives the year. Wikipedia gives no year. A search result listed Giant Bomb's entry as 2001 (not opened).
- **Little Sound DJ used by musicians today:** Only the developer's site and a 2016 article were checked. No independent 2025 or 2026 source was found.
- **Ultimate Soundtracker publisher:** Wikipedia's Music tracker article credits EAS Computer Technik. The Ultimate Soundtracker article names no publisher.
- **Ultimate Soundtracker is the first music tracker:** This is Wikipedia's claim only. Other sources were not checked.
- **Game Boy sales figure of 118.69 million (Nintendo table):** The figure was read from image text, and Wikipedia's body text ties the same number to 2003 (BusinessWeek). Check the Nintendo table by eye before using.
- **NES Europe launch year:** Wikipedia's infobox says 1 September 1986 (Scandinavia) and 1987 for most of Europe. Its body text says "early 1986". The conflict is unresolved.
- **NES full North American release in February 1986:** A search summary said this. Wikipedia gives 27 September 1986, which is what this brief uses.
- **Average adult hearing up to 20,000 Hz:** The Hertz article gives 16,000 Hz for the average adult, which conflicts with the Hearing range article. Both are noted above.
- **Who coined the word "chiptune", and when:** No source was found.
- **Commodore 64 "best-selling single computer model":** Wikipedia says "best-selling desktop computer model" (Guinness). The stronger wording is not used.
