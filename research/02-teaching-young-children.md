# Teaching chiptunes to a 3-year-old and a 6-year-old: research brief

Prepared 11 October 2026. Quotes were checked against the page or PDF they come from. Our own suggestions are marked **[our suggestion]**. Claims I could not verify are marked **UNVERIFIED** and listed again at the end.

## At a glance

- **Beat.** A 3-year-old is encouraged to clap or tap to the pulse ([Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters)). Steady-beat keeping matures later; research points to a major step at 6 to 8 ([Kertész 2023](https://ppk.elte.hu/dstore/document/1327/kertesz_csaba_tezisfuzet_angol.pdf)). Design for big, simple beats.
- **Pitch.** Keep melodies in a small range. Music Mark gives a comfortable range for young children of D to B ([Music Mark](https://www.musicmark.org.uk/a-common-approach/early-years/area-d/age-3-4/d2/)), and Development Matters asks adults to use a limited pitch range ([Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters).
- **Five-note melodies.** Pentatonic (five-note) melodies suit improvising, because any note sounds acceptable against the others ([Combs 2012](https://paulcombs.com/Word_Press/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/StrtngOffImprvsrsWebVer.html)). This is a teaching claim, not a child study.
- **Touch.** Tap only for the youngest. In one study, only 27% of 2- and 3-year-olds could tap an intended spot ([Yadav et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3345/cep.2019.00997)). Make targets big and tolerate stray touches.
- **Words.** The youngest could not follow on-screen prompts in that same study ([Yadav et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3345/cep.2019.00997)). Use icons plus a short spoken line.
- **Screen time.** UK guidance (March 2026) says for 2- to 5-year-olds: "Try to keep it to 1 hour a day. Less if possible." ([Best Start in Life](https://www.beststartinlife.gov.uk/screen-time-under-5s)). Watch together.

## 2. What children can do musically

### 2a. Three-year-olds

- **Steady beat is still developing.** Development Matters encourages it from 3 (see 1a). A 2014 study tapped 20 three-year-olds (mean age 3.33) and 20 five-year-olds (mean age 5.42) in synchrony with a 200 ms sound. The authors describe a window beyond which "the rhythm is lost and the synchronization becomes difficult". [Bobin-Bègue, Droit-Volet and Provasi 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01346/full)
- **The big leap comes later.** A 2023 thesis summary says "a major developmental leap between the ages of 6-8 years", after which spontaneous tempo "approaches the adult level of around 100 bpm". It adds that this rests on cross-sectional data. [Kertész 2023 thesis summary (ELTE)](https://ppk.elte.hu/dstore/document/1327/kertesz_csaba_tezisfuzet_angol.pdf)
- **The same thesis on complex music.** "for preschool and early school age groups, complex music is more appropriate, providing a natural and motivating task situation." [Kertész 2023](https://ppk.elte.hu/dstore/document/1327/kertesz_csaba_tezisfuzet_angol.pdf)
- **Loves moving to music.** "move and dance to music" and "respond emotionally and physically to music when it changes" (Birth to 3). [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters)
- **Loud/quiet, fast/slow, high/low.** Development Matters gives these as examples: "Play and perform music with different: dynamics (loud and quiet), tempo (fast and slow), pitch (high and low), rhythms (pattern of sound)". [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters)
- **Repeating patterns.** "make rhythmical and repetitive sounds" and "anticipate phrases and actions in rhymes and songs" (Birth to 3). [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters)
- **Singing range is small.** Music Mark: "Because young children have very short vocal folds, they can comfortably sing a limited pitch range of D to B." and "Start with using soh-mi songs... to develop pitch-matching skills." [Music Mark, A Common Approach, age 3-4](https://www.musicmark.org.uk/a-common-approach/early-years/area-d/age-3-4/d2/). Youth Music: "When supporting children to develop their singing voice use a limited pitch range". [Youth Music](https://www.youthmusic.org.uk/resources/vocalising-and-singing)
- **Attention.** DiCarlo et al. (2016) studied 63 preschool-age children under child-choice, adult-choice and adult-presentation teaching. The abstract reports "a statistically significant difference between the teaching conditions". The page gives no mean durations. [DiCarlo et al. 2016 (LSU record)](https://repository.lsu.edu/education_pubs/38)
- **The "few minutes" figure is UNVERIFIED.** I found no research figure. I also found no research behind the "2-3 minutes per year of age" rule of thumb, which circulates on parenting sites. **[our suggestion]** Design rounds the child can start and stop at any point.

### 2b. Six-year-olds (Year 2, aged 6 to 7)

- **Keep a steady beat.** Year 1: "Walk, move or clap a steady beat with others" (p.15). Year 2: "Walk in time to the beat of a piece of music or song" (p.18). [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)
- **Beat-keeping still maturing.** Beat synchronisation is still developing at this age, and the big leap comes at 6 to 8 (see 2a). [Kertész 2023](https://ppk.elte.hu/dstore/document/1327/kertesz_csaba_tezisfuzet_angol.pdf)
- **Copy short rhythms.** "Perform short copycat rhythm patterns accurately, led by the teacher" (Year 1, p.15). "Play copycat rhythms, copying a leader" (Year 2, p.18). [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)
- **High/low and up/down.** Year 1: "comparing high and low sounds" (p.15). Year 2: "Respond independently to pitch changes... (e.g. stand up/sit down, hands high/hands low)" (p.18). [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)
- **Invent short tunes.** Year 1: "Invent, retain and recall rhythm and pitch patterns and perform these for others, taking turns" (p.14). Year 2: "Work with a partner to improvise simple question and answer phrases" (p.17). [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)
- **Simple graphic notation.** Year 1: "Recognise how graphic notation can represent created sounds. Explore and invent own symbols" (p.14). Year 2: "Use graphic symbols, dot notation and stick notation" (p.17). [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)
- **Music training and motor control.** A 2024 study of 74 children aged 6 to 7 found that children with at least six months of music training "had a higher level of motor inhibitory control" on a stand-still task. It is cross-sectional, so it cannot show cause. [Bayanova et al. 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1333580/full)

## 3. Teaching moves that work at these ages

Each entry gives the support and a concrete app example. App examples are **[our suggestion]**.

### 3.1 Call-and-response echo games

- Support: "Sing call-and-response songs, so that children can echo phrases of songs you sing." Also "Use songs with and without words – children may pitch-match more easily without words. Try using one-syllable sounds such as 'ba'." [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters). Year 2 partner question-and-answer work: [MMC p.17](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf).
- App: the app plays a two-note or three-note "question" phrase; the child taps the matching "answer" on a grid. Keep phrases to two to four notes.

### 3.2 Body moves for high and low

- Support: "hands high/hands low" for pitch (Year 2, [MMC p.18](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)); Development Matters lists pitch (high and low) as a musical contrast to play with (see 2a).
- App: a sprite stretches up for a high note and crouches for a low note. Add the spoken words "high" and "low".

### 3.3 Freeze dance (stop and start)

- Support: "Play movement and listening games that use different sounds for different movements. Suggestions include march to the sound of the drum or creep to the sound of the maraca." [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters).
- Research: a 2025 study (Preston et al.) "divided 111 four- and five-year-olds into four groups". It found that a "simple 12-minute dance game" improved an executive-function task immediately. The freeze-dance group did best on those tasks. We know this from a news summary; the paper itself was not opened. [Boldscience summary, 20 October 2025](https://boldscience.org/?p=26001)
- App: music plays and the character dances; when the music stops, the character freezes and waits. Tap to restart. Avoid tap-and-hold. [our suggestion]

### 3.4 Marching to the beat

- Support: "march to the sound of the drum" (Development Matters, see 3.3). Year 1: "Walk, move or clap a steady beat with others" (p.15). [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)
- App: a large pulse bar; a character steps on each beat; the child taps a big pad on the beat. [our suggestion]

### 3.5 Pentatonic improvisation ("no wrong notes")

- Support: "Include pentatonic songs" (Year 1, [MMC p.13](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)). Schott, publisher of Orff's *Music for Children*: "when children discover, invent, improvise and compose, their experience of music is intensified." Volume I contains "instrumental pieces and melodic exercises using only the five note scale." [Schott](https://www.schott-music.com/en/music-for-children-noc35165.html)
- A teacher's 2012 article (written for adults and students, not children): "Even a random arrangement of the notes will have a pleasing sound", which lets students focus on rhythm "without worrying about 'wrong' notes". The pentatonic is formed by omitting "the fourth and seventh notes", with "no half steps". [Combs 2012](https://paulcombs.com/Word_Press/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/StrtngOffImprvsrsWebVer.html)
- Caveat: "no wrong notes" is a teaching shorthand. I found no child study of it. **UNVERIFIED** as a child-learning claim.
- App: a five-note grid (for example C, D, E, G, A). Keep the backing in one key. [our suggestion]

### 3.6 Graphic scores with pictures and colours

- Support: "Follow pictures and symbols to guide singing and playing, e.g. 4 dots = 4 taps on the drum." (Year 1, [MMC p.15](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)).
- Research: in a small case study of four 5-year-olds, the children "use coloured geometrical shapes on paper and a sequence of building blocks" to represent music. "the children are able to communicate their understanding of the relationship between representation (sign) and sound." [Pramling 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-music-education/article/abs/external-representation-and-the-architecture-of-music-children-inventing-and-speaking-about-notations/11E6AD7189BDA6CD03A3200EF62BF340)
- App: one picture per sound (a bird for a high sound, a drum for a low one), with dots for repeats. [our suggestion]

### 3.7 Naming sounds with descriptive words (buzzy, soft, hissy)

- Support: "respond to what they have heard, expressing their thoughts and feelings" (3-4s, [Development Matters](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters)). Reception: "Help them to define colours, shapes, textures and smells in their own words." (same source, art context). Year 1 uses sounds for stories: "quiet sounds created on a rainstick/shakers to depict a shower" (p.15, [MMC](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6061f833d3bf7f5ce1060a90/Model_Music_Curriculum_Full.pdf)).
- Research gap: **UNVERIFIED** that any study shows which timbre words young children use or understand. The word list is a design choice.
- App: each sound has a picture, a spoken word and a short demo. [our suggestion]

## 4. Try-it-at-home sound experiments

Safety notes marked **[our note]** are our additions. The source did not give them.

### 4.1 Rice on cling film ("see the sound")

- Needs: bowl, cling film, tape or an elastic band, a handful of rice, a drum or a pan and spoon.
- Steps: (1) Stretch cling film over the bowl and fasten it. (2) Scatter rice on the film. (3) Hold the drum close to the bowl and hit it hard, then watch the rice.
- Shows: "The clingfilm stretched over the bowl is similar to your eardrum." The vibration moves the film and the rice jumps.
- Safety: the source lists none. **[our note]** Rice is a small item; keep it on the table and away from mouths. An adult does the drumming. The page is written for ages 7 to 10.
- Source: [Science Sparks, "How can you see sound?"](https://www.science-sparks.com/how-can-you-see-sound)

### 4.2 Rubber bands on a box (tight = high, loose = low)

- Needs: tissue box or shoebox, 4 to 6 rubber bands of different thicknesses, optional paper towel tube.
- Steps: (1) Stretch the bands across the box opening. (2) Pluck each band gently, then firmly. (3) Tighten or loosen one band and listen.
- Shows: "Thinner or tighter rubber bands vibrate faster and produce higher-pitched sounds." "Thicker or looser rubber bands vibrate more slowly and produce lower-pitched sounds."
- Safety: none listed. **[our note]** Bands can snap; an adult fits them and keeps them away from faces.
- Source: [Little Bins for Little Hands, Tissue Box Guitar](https://littlebinsforlittlehands.com/tissue-box-guitar-sound-science-experiment-vibrations-pitch) (Grades K to 4)

### 4.3 Glasses with different water levels

- Needs: 3 identical glasses or jars, a jug of water, a pencil, optional food colouring.
- Steps: (1) Set the glasses apart so a child can tap one without touching its neighbour. (2) Pour a little water into one glass and almost fill another. (3) Tap gently with the pencil and compare the sounds and the water levels.
- Shows: "the glass with just a little bit of water makes a higher-pitch sound, and the full glass makes a lower-pitch sound." (Ten minutes.)
- Safety: none listed. **[our note]** Glass can break. Use plastic cups for a 3-year-old, or let the grown-up do the tapping.
- Source: [Twin Cities PBS, "Sounds High and Low" (home activity, 2021)](https://cms-assets.prod.pbskids.org/parents/KindsOfSounds_Activity_HighLow_Home.pdf)

### 4.4 Pots-and-pans drum kit

- Needs: lightweight metal pots and pans, wooden and metal spoons, a soft mat.
- Steps (ages 1 to 4): (1) Together, tap out simple rhythms or beats. (2) Repeat the rhythms. (3) Copy each other and talk about the sounds. [Portsmouth Education Partnership card](https://www.portsmoutheducationpartnership.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Pots-and-pans.pdf)
- Soft and loud: "Tap lightly and say, 'Listen! That was a soft sound.' Tap harder and say, 'That was a loud sound!'" [Old Station Nursery](https://www.theoldstationnursery.co.uk/resource/pot-and-pan-drumming-at-home-for-babies/)
- Safety: "Supervise at all times—especially when using utensils." "Choose lightweight, baby-safe materials (no heavy pots or sharp edges)." "Ensure a safe space where nothing can tip over." [Old Station Nursery](https://www.theoldstationnursery.co.uk/resource/pot-and-pan-drumming-at-home-for-babies/) (written for babies, so apply the same care for a 3-year-old)

### 4.5 Hand on the throat while humming

- Needs: nothing.
- Steps (Irish 1st-class lesson): (1) "Put a gentle hand on your throat and hum a quiet tune. What can you feel?" (2) Keep humming and notice the buzz. (3) Stop and check whether the feeling stops.
- Shows: the throat vibrates while humming; the feeling stops when the humming stops.
- Safety: none listed. **[our note]** Keep the humming gentle. The page gives no age; first class is usually 6 to 7.
- Source: [Coding Ireland, "Sound Is a Wobble"](https://codingireland.ie/ViewLesson/sound-is-a-wobble-20712/8265)

### 4.6 Paper-cup and string telephone

- Needs: 2 cups, about 10 feet of string, a pencil or pin to make holes.
- Steps: (1) Poke a small hole in the bottom of each cup. (2) Thread the string through each cup and tie a large knot inside. (3) Walk apart until the string is tight; one talks, the other listens; then switch.
- Shows: "When the string is pulled tight, the vibrations propagate along it as mechanical energy." A loose or touched string interrupts the vibrations.
- Safety: the source has no safety section. **[our note]** An adult makes the holes. String can be a choking or strangulation risk for toddlers, so keep it short and tight, and supervise closely.
- Source: [Little Bins for Little Hands, Cup and String Telephone](https://littlebinsforlittlehands.com/cup-and-string-telephone-sound-science/) (Grades K to 4)

## 5. Touch-screen design for 3- and 6-year-olds

### 5.1 Target sizes

- **NN/g (Aurora Harley, 5 May 2019).** Minimum size "1cm × 1cm (0.4in x 0.4in)". "To avoid accidental taps, targets must first be big enough, and then also spaced well enough." "Young children require large, easy-to-reach controls because their physical skills are not as developed as those of adults." The article gives no child-specific number. [NN/g, Touch Targets on Touchscreens](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)
- **Google (Android accessibility).** "a width and height of at least 48dp" and "separated by 8dp of space or more". [Google, Touch target size](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858?hl=en)
- **Apple (iOS), as quoted by Adrian Roselli (2019, updated 2024).** "For iOS, it recommends 44 points × 44 points (not pixels) as a minimum." [Roselli, Target Size and 2.5.5](https://adrianroselli.com/2019/06/target-size-and-2-5-5.html). Apple's own guidelines page would not load in my tools.
- **Children's accuracy on touch targets.** Brown and Anthony (CHI EIST 2012), eight children and six adults, 43 targets of varying size and location: "children's touches were not within the target 46% of the time, whereas adults only missed 32% of the time." Their conclusion: "children tend to miss targets more often than adults, indicating a need for greater tolerance in location of touch". [Brown and Anthony 2012](https://lisa-anthony.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/brown-and-anthony-chi2012eist.pdf). The children's ages are not clear in the text I could read.
- **Preschoolers pointing (mouse, not touch).** Hourcade, Bederson, Druin and Guimbretière (2003): 13 four-year-olds, 13 five-year-olds and 13 adults. "target size had a significant effect on accuracy and target reentry." [Hourcade et al. 2003](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/items/f3e5dc45-7acc-40c6-8dfd-a258b9c0bc9b/full)
- **[our suggestion]** Treat 48dp and 1 cm as floors, not targets. Make the main buttons much bigger, with wide gaps, and test with a real 3-year-old.

### 5.2 Tap, drag and swipe

- **Yadav et al. (2020), New Delhi, 90 children aged 2 to 8, July 2019.** "Two- and 3-year-old children could not follow any prompting technique and only a minority (27%) could tap the touchscreen at an intended place." Four- to 6-year-olds could do "a tap and slide (57%)". Drag-and-drop was reported only for 7- and 8-year-olds ("dragging and dropping (30%)"). [Yadav et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3345/cep.2019.00997)
- **Sesame Workshop (2012).** "Tapping is one of the most intuitive interactions for preschoolers while pinching and flicking are often difficult". [Sesame Workshop, Best Practices: Designing Touch Tablet Experiences for Preschoolers](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=19607)
- **Rule for the youngest.** Tap only. Drag is not shown to work for under-4s in this evidence. **[our suggestion]** Where drag is essential, use a tap-to-pick-up, tap-to-place route instead.

### 5.3 Double tap and long press

- **Nácher Soler et al. (2014), "Improving Pre-Kindergarten Touch Performance" (ACM).** Double tap has "some issues related to spurious entry events", and long press has "time-constrained interactions". Guideline-based versions had "a positive effect on success rates". The record gives only "pre-kindergarten" as the age. [Nácher Soler et al. 2014 (RiuNet record; DOI 10.1145/2669485.2669498)](https://riunet.upv.es/handle/10251/65315?show=full)
- **[our suggestion]** Make a single tap do everything a child must do. Use double tap and long press as optional shortcuts, if at all.

### 5.4 Immediate feedback and prompts

- Sesame Workshop: "Provide dialogue and visual reinforcements to help preschoolers who usually cannot read." [Sesame Workshop](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=19607)
- Yadav et al.: four- to six-year-olds could follow "instructions provided through animation (63%)". [Yadav et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3345/cep.2019.00997)
- **[our suggestion]** Every tap gets an immediate sound and movement. Give instructions as animation plus a spoken line, not text.

### 5.5 No reliance on reading

- Sesame Workshop (quoted above): preschoolers "usually cannot read". [Sesame Workshop](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=19607)
- Yadav et al.: "Apps for children in this age group should work with little intervention from the children and may ignore accidental touches on the screen." (For the 2- and 3-year-old group.) [Yadav et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3345/cep.2019.00997)
- **[our suggestion]** Icons and voice. Ignore stray touches. No timed reactions. One task per screen.

### 5.6 Hands and layout

- Sesame Workshop: "Preschoolers tend to rest their wrists along the bottom edge of tablets". [Sesame Workshop](https://joanganzcooneycenter.org/?p=19607)
- **[our suggestion]** Keep important buttons away from the bottom edge.

### 5.7 Limiting choices

- **UNVERIFIED.** I could not open NN/g's "Designing for Kids: Cognitive Considerations". Blog sources suggest three or four options per screen, but they are not research.
- **[our suggestion]** Show two to four choices at a time, one decision per screen.

## 6. Screen time in the UK (for the grown-ups' notes)

The UK government guidance for under-5s (Best Start in Life, published 26 March 2026, last updated 29 June 2026) says that for under-2s, screens are for "shared activities with family that encourage bonding, interaction and conversation". For 2- to 5-year-olds it says "Try to keep it to 1 hour a day. Less if possible." It advises keeping bedrooms and mealtimes free from screens, avoiding screens in the hour before bed, and avoiding young children using screens alone. It also recommends watching and discussing content together. The page gives no advice on session length. The Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health welcomed the guidance: "Today's guidance is a much welcome resource to help parents protect those short, but developmentally crucial early years." Sources: [Best Start in Life](https://www.beststartinlife.gov.uk/screen-time-under-5s); [DfE and DHSC press release, 26 March 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/new-screen-time-guidance-for-parents-of-under-5s); [RCPCH, 27 March 2026](https://www.rcpch.ac.uk/news-events/news/2026-03/rcpch-welcomes-screen-time-guidance-under-5s).
